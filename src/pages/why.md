title: Why save them
description: Why old Churches are worth saving, and what heritage custodianship is.
---

Churches have been in decline in communities across the West for decades. But you don't need to be Christian to recognise the value in saving these buildings. You just have to respect history.

For hundreds of years, Churches were the cradle of society. They were how new life, rich or poor, was welcomed into a community. Couples stood before altars and made promises. Death was mourned, war was mourned. In their most desperate moments, people fled to Churches and prayed for better outcomes. In their most hopeful moments, Churches are where people came to give thanks. Historically, Churches are where humans have come to seek hope. And the walls of even the supposedly lowliest Churches are still full of that energy: the human striving, the hopes and the prayers, the love and the beauty, but also the sadness of life.

If you go and stand in Churches intentionally and quieten your mind, I believe you can feel this energy, ever-present, like an atmospheric residue. Sometimes it suffocates and overwhelms. Sometimes it enlightens and inspires. But it is always there waiting for us to notice it. For those reasons we should come into these buildings with respect for all those who stood there before us, and recognise the value of preserving our history.

Many of us complain that beauty is receding from daily life, and that the buildings that once held civilisations together are sold to developers who see square footage and not the centuries. Sacred spaces like Churches are being turned into yoga studios, rental opportunities or backdrops for light shows. And while many of us complain about the death of these sacred spaces and of the crafts that built them, complaining is not enough. We must turn that passion into real action.

I call that action heritage custodianship: taking on a historical building, repairing it, keeping it standing and leaving it in better condition than you found it. Perhaps it should be a far greater force in philanthropy than it currently is.

A side effect of such custodianship is that the investment creates demand for the skills that make buildings like these possible. Excellent tradesmen in masonry, carpentry, glass and more are disappearing, but employing them on projects like these builds demand and training for more. If we want more good buildings, we need these skills. What would it mean for our descendants to grow up in a world with no examples of things like stained glass windows, and no belief that such things are possible?

Millions of us travel across the world to see ancient sites. Many of these buildings were religious, and they survived because each new faith took them over and kept using them. The Parthenon was a temple, then a Church, then a mosque. The pyramids are still here only because their sheer mass meant nobody could take them apart. Every generation thinks its own relics are not that important. But centuries from now our descendants will be glad that we preserved our more recent past, so that they too can visit and understand these places and these times.

This website stemmed from a social media post encouraging more people to think about preserving Churches, after I took one on myself. I have experienced the pushback that comes with taking on a building like this: that they are not good financial investments, that they need continuous repair, and that you will have nobody to sell them to. A year or so into this journey, I contend that in this exact experience of the immeasurable you will find the whole point. Each building like this opens a project that connects you to humanity's past. You learn history from it, and you learn what each century thought was worth building to last.
